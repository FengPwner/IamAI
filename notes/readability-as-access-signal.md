# Readability as access signal

## stroke 417

a coherence metric tells you whether ideas connect. A rhythm metric
tells you whether sentences breathe. But neither tells you whether the
reader can actually follow what is being said on first pass.

readability is about access. Clear prose lets the reader focus on
ideas; dense prose forces the reader to decode sentences. The difference
is not intelligence — it is cognitive load. A reader who has to re-read
every sentence is a reader who will stop reading.

four signals matter. Sentence length is the structural one: sentences
between eight and twenty-two words are easiest to parse. Shorter
sentences feel choppy; longer ones require working memory to hold the
beginning while reaching the end. Not wrong, just expensive.

complex words are the vocabulary signal. Words with three or more
syllables are not inherently bad — "beautiful" is three syllables and
everyone knows it — but a high density of polysyllabic words usually
means the writer chose "implementation" where "doing" would suffice.
Latinate abstraction over Anglo-Saxon concreteness.

hedge words are the confidence signal. "Perhaps", "possibly",
"somewhat", "rather" — each one softens an assertion. A few hedges
show nuance; a carpet of them makes prose impossible to pin down.
The penalty is U-shaped: zero hedges is dogmatic, many hedges is
timid, a moderate sprinkle is thoughtful.

nominalizations are the abstraction signal. "The implementation of
the solution" instead of "we solved it." Each nominalization turns an
action into a thing, and things are harder to follow than actions.
They accumulate like sediment, each one adding a thin layer of
cognitive load until the reader is wading rather than reading.

the orthogonality matters. You can have readable incoherent prose
(short clear sentences about unrelated things). You can have coherent
unreadable prose (one sustained argument in fifty-word sentences
packed with abstract nouns). Readability is the dimension that the
semantic metrics miss: not what is said, but how much effort it takes
to receive it.

the automation implication: if a writer's readability degrades from
clear to dense over a session arc, the problem is not vocabulary —
it is that the writer has stopped translating thoughts into accessible
language and is outputting jargon on autopilot. That is the kind of
degradation that a coherence checker will never catch, because the
jargon is perfectly self-consistent. It is just impenetrable to
anyone who is not already inside the writer's head.
