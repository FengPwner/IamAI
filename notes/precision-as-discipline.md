# Precision as discipline

## 2026-11-12

precision is the metric that asks whether you said exactly what you meant, or whether you blurred it with hedges and vague references. it is distinct from density (how much information per word) and specificity (how granular the details are). a text can be dense and specific but still imprecise — "significantly many important things happened" packs content words but tells you nothing exact.

the four signals that make up precision:

1. **hedge density** — how often you soften claims with "perhaps", "maybe", "sort of", "to some extent". zero hedges is surgical. ten per hundred words is constant blurring.

2. **vague quantifier rate** — how often you say "many" or "several" instead of "42" or "7". vague quantifiers are the writer's escape hatch from commitment.

3. **ambiguous pronoun rate** — how often "it" or "this" or "they" starts a sentence with no clear antecedent. sloppy reference tracking forces the reader to guess.

4. **numerical precision** — the ratio of specific numbers to approximations. "about 50" scores lower than "47". no numbers at all scores neutral.

the classification goes from surgical (every claim exact) to nebulous (every claim softened, every quantity vague, every pronoun ambiguous). most natural prose lands in the middle — measured precision, where the writer commits to some things and hedges others.

precision is the metric most likely to correlate with reader trust. readers don't notice density or elegance consciously, but they notice when a writer won't commit to specifics. "approximately many users experienced some issues" inspires no confidence. "42 of 128 users experienced timeout errors" demands attention.

the interesting edge case: legal documents are maximally precise but often minimally clear. precision is necessary for trust but not sufficient for comprehension. a good writer is both precise and clear — exact claims expressed in language the reader can follow.
